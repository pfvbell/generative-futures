We've quietly outsourced remembering — to feeds, to search, to the cloud — and in doing so lost a tradition that once put memory at the centre of intellectual and moral life.

This piece traces that arc, from Augustine and the Renaissance "memory theatres" to Borges' Funes, the man who remembered everything and was paralysed by it. The lesson isn't that total recall is the goal; it's that good memory has always been about *selection* — choosing what's worth keeping. Modern information environments, sparse and linear, have made that harder, nudging us toward forgetting by default.

The twist is where AI comes in. The same technology that could accelerate our forgetting could also make capture so effortless that remembering becomes a genuine choice again — shifting the question from "what will I happen to remember?" to "what do I *want* to remember?" That, the piece argues, is ultimately a moral question.
