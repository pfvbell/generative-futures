Carl Frey's "How Progress Ends" is a big-picture history of why some societies keep innovating and others stall, and this review uses it to think about the US–China technology race.

Frey's core idea is a sequencing argument: you need decentralised, networked exploration to *discover* technologies, and centralised coordination to *scale* them, and the trouble comes when a society gets the order wrong, or gets stuck in one mode. The review walks through his historical case studies, from Britain's Lunar Society to Meiji Japan to the Soviet Union, and applies the lens to today: a US whose decentralised edge is eroding under lobbying and NIMBYism, and a China that risks recentralising away from the pragmatism that powered its rise.

It's an admiring but critical read. The sharpest pushback: Frey's clean split between "innovation" and "old technology" underrates how much real innovation happens in *deployment*, exactly what DeepSeek showed.
