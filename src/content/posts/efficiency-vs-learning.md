There's a hidden cost to AI's greatest selling point. The faster a chatbot hands you a finished answer, the less of the thinking you do yourself — and thinking is how we learn.

This essay builds on a neat University of Pennsylvania experiment: give two groups the exact same information, one via ChatGPT-style summary and one via search-engine links, and the chatbot group learns less, feels less ownership, and produces advice that others trust less. The difference isn't the information; it's the *friction*. Synthesising scattered sources is effortful, and that effort is the learning.

The point isn't that AI makes us stupid. It's that "efficient" and "educational" are different design goals, and right now the incentives push everything toward efficiency. Tools that deliberately add friction — Socratic modes, partial answers — could choose differently. As ever, the outcome is a design choice, not a destiny.
